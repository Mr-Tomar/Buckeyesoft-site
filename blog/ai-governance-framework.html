AI Governance for Growing Companies: The Policies to Put in Place First

By BuckEyeSoft Team·October 18, 2026·7 min read

Your employees are already using AI, whether or not you have a policy. Governance isn't about stopping that. It is about making sure the AI your business relies on is secure, accurate and accountable, so you can scale it with confidence instead of anxiety.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a short acceptable use policy that people will actually read.
  • Classify AI use cases by risk and match controls to each tier.
  • Keep humans accountable for high-impact decisions.
  • Monitor AI systems in production just like any other critical service.

Why governance matters now

Three forces make AI governance urgent for growing companies. Regulation is expanding, with the EU AI Act, US state laws and sector rules all setting expectations. Customers and partners increasingly ask how you use AI in security questionnaires. And agentic systems that take actions raise the stakes of mistakes. A lightweight framework now is far cheaper than retrofitting one later.

1. An acceptable use policy

Write one or two pages that cover which AI tools are approved, what data may and may not be entered into them, how AI-generated content must be reviewed and who to ask when unsure. Keep it practical, with examples. Update it every quarter as tools change.

2. Clear data rules

Define which data classifications can be used with which AI services. Public and internal data might be fine with approved enterprise tools; confidential customer or health data may need private deployments, extra contracts or be off-limits. Make sure vendors do not train their models on your data unless you explicitly agree.

3. Risk tiers for use cases

Not every AI use case needs the same scrutiny. A simple three-tier model works well:

  • Low risk: drafting internal content, summarising meetings. Standard policy applies.
  • Medium risk: customer-facing assistants, internal decision support. Requires testing, monitoring and a named owner.
  • High risk: decisions affecting employment, credit, health or legal rights, or agents that act on financial systems. Requires formal review, human approval and documented testing for bias and accuracy.

4. Human oversight and accountability

Every AI system needs a named business owner who is accountable for its outcomes. For higher-risk tiers, decide where a person must review or approve outputs before they take effect. This is especially important for AI agents that can act on systems.

5. Vendor and model review

Before adopting an AI product, check where data is stored and processed, retention terms, security certifications, how the vendor handles incidents and whether you can export your data. Add these questions to your standard procurement process.

6. Monitoring and incident response

Track quality, cost and usage for production AI systems. Give users an easy way to flag bad outputs. Define what counts as an AI incident, such as a data leak, a harmful response or a wrong action, and route it through your existing incident process.

7. Security controls specific to AI

Protect against prompt injection, restrict which tools and data each AI system can reach, log prompts and actions for audit and red-team important systems before launch. These sit alongside, not instead of, your existing security controls in your cloud environment.

Getting started

You do not need a large committee. A small working group from IT, security, legal and one or two business leaders can draft the first version of these policies in a few weeks.
